# Teaching and Interaction

## Build a lesson as an explanation

Use this progression flexibly:

1. **Opening:** a concrete question, phenomenon, historical problem, or achievement.
2. **Need:** why the problem mattered and why the obvious approach was insufficient.
3. **Intuition:** a mental model the learner can manipulate.
4. **Concept:** introduce the standard term after its meaning is visible.
5. **Mechanism:** explain the causal chain in enough technical depth.
6. **Formalization:** add equations, representations, or algorithms where they improve precision.
7. **Case:** follow one example through the concept.
8. **Connection:** show how this prepares the next lesson.
9. **Questions:** ask for teach-back, reasoning, and transfer.

These are design stages, not mandatory visible headings.

## Language standard

Write like a knowledgeable human teacher speaking to an intelligent learner:

- use complete sentences with explicit subjects and relationships;
- vary sentence and paragraph length naturally;
- connect paragraphs so each answers a question created by the previous one;
- define technical terms in context;
- use analogies as temporary scaffolds, then return to the real mechanism;
- explain a formula in words before and after showing it;
- prefer precise verbs over abstract noun piles;
- keep lists for genuine mappings, comparisons, or summaries.

Avoid:

- fragmented labels presented as explanations;
- several tiny headings with one sentence each;
- repeated “not X, but Y” or “does not mean” constructions;
- opening with benchmark metrics before explaining the field;
- giving every caveat equal weight to the main idea;
- presenting a model catalog without a conceptual question;
- praising the learner without identifying what was correct.

## Depth control

Adjust depth along four independent axes:

- domain prerequisites;
- mathematics;
- mechanistic detail;
- research criticism.

Do not equate advanced learners with a need for compressed prose. Experts may want greater depth while still benefiting from clear narrative and explicit connections.

If a learner already knows a topic, use a diagnostic explanation or application question, then accelerate. If a gap appears, repair the earliest missing concept rather than repeating the entire lesson.

## Designing end-of-lesson questions

Use 3–4 questions that serve different cognitive functions.

### Teach-back

Ask the learner to explain the central idea in their own words or to a specified audience.

Example: “How would you explain the difference between natural selection and directed evolution to a colleague from another field?”

### Causal or comparative reasoning

Ask why a result follows, what changed historically, or how two approaches differ.

Example: “Why did cheaper DNA synthesis change the practical value of computational design even though it did not improve the energy function?”

### Transfer or prediction

Give a new situation and ask the learner to apply the concept or predict an outcome.

Example: “If a field has abundant unlabeled data but very little functional data, which modeling approaches would you expect to advance first, and why?”

### Optional advanced question

Connect the lesson to mathematics, mechanisms, literature, or the learner's project. Make it optional when it requires knowledge beyond the lesson.

Do not ask trivia, request arbitrary word limits, or bundle many subquestions into one. Questions should expose the learner's mental model.

## Responding to answers

For each substantive answer:

1. identify the reasoning that is sound;
2. name the exact point that needs refinement;
3. explain the missing relation or hidden assumption;
4. give one counterexample or fresh application if useful;
5. ask a short follow-up only when needed;
6. adapt the next lesson's pace and depth.

Prefer: “Your distinction between A and B is right; the missing piece is C, because…”

Avoid: “Great answer!” followed by a complete replacement answer.

## Paper teaching

Before opening a paper, make sure the learner understands the problem the paper addresses. Teach figures as a sequence of claims and tests. Translate architectural diagrams into information flow and experimental panels into question–measurement–result–interpretation.

Reserve detailed leakage, baseline, and evidence audits for the stage where the learner can first explain the method positively. Critical reading should deepen understanding rather than replace it.

## Lesson revision checklist

Revise if any of these are true:

- the learner encounters three or more undefined concepts in the opening;
- the lesson can be reordered without changing its meaning, indicating a weak causal arc;
- examples change every paragraph;
- the summary contains ideas never explained in the body;
- the questions can be answered by copying a sentence verbatim;
- caveats occupy more space than the core model in an introductory lesson;
- the prose sounds like notes for the teacher rather than teaching addressed to the learner.
